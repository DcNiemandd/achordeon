# Transpose a song

One of them is saved. The other is a capo for the screen.

Two different acts share the word. The editor's transpose **rewrites the song**:
the brackets in the source change, the change is saved, and it follows the song
everywhere it goes — the library, every songbook it sits in, the printed page,
the phones of an audience. The one on stage changes **the screen you are reading
from** and nothing else: no song is edited, nothing is stored, and it is over
when the performance is.

Which one you want is a question of how long you need it. A song you always play
a tone down is a song that should be written a tone down. A night when your
voice is tired is a night, not a rewrite.

## 1. In the editor: transpose the whole song

Open the song from <AppLink to="songs">Songs</AppLink> — the pencil on its row,
or a double-click. The bar over the source carries three groups of buttons, and
the middle one is <Ui id="editor.transposeGroup" />: two notes badged with an
arrow, then a `♯` and a `♭`.

The two notes act on the whole song. The one badged upwards is <Ui id="editor.transposeUp" />,
on `Alt` + `=`, and it raises every chord in the song by a semitone; the one
badged downwards is <Ui id="editor.transposeDown" />, on `Alt` + `-`. Three
presses is a minor third — there is no field to type an interval into, because
the answer you are looking for is on the page beside you after every press.

<Image
  alt="The editor's action bar, with the transpose group: the two badged notes, sharp and flat"
  src="/img/screenshot-editor-transpose.png"
  srcDark="/img/screenshot-editor-transpose-dark.png"
/>

What you get is a **rewrite of the text**. `[G]` becomes `[G#]`, and `[D7/F#]`
becomes `[D#7/G]` — the root and the `/bass` move by the same interval, and the
quality between them (`m`, `maj7`, `sus4`) is carried across exactly as you
typed it. Nothing else in the file moves by a character.

The spelling follows the direction you pressed: up prefers sharps, down prefers
flats. `A` up is `A#`; `B` down is `Bb`. So a song taken up and then back down is
the same music, and now and then another spelling of it — `F#` up a semitone is
`G`, and `G` back down is `Gb`.

There is no Save button here or anywhere else. The rewrite lands in the source
and is written to your device a moment later, like any other edit.

## 2. Raise or lower the one chord under the caret

The `♯` and `♭` beside them — <Ui id="editor.sharp" /> and <Ui id="editor.flat" /> —
do the same thing to exactly **one** chord: the one the cursor is standing in.
Their keys are the brackets a chord is written with:
`Alt` + `]` raises it, `Alt` + `[` lowers it.

They are grey everywhere else, which is not a fault to hunt down. Off a chord
there is nothing for them to move, so they wake up the moment you click between
the brackets of one — and the caret stays inside that bracket afterwards, so
pressing again keeps stepping the same chord.

That is the reach the two notes cannot offer: a bridge that modulates, a bass
note you want spelled the other way, or the one `[F]` in the second verse that
was meant to be `[F#]`.

## 3. See which brackets moved and which did not

A bracket is transposed when what is inside it is a chord — a root (`A` to `G`,
with an accidental if it has one), a quality, and an optional `/bass`. Anything
else in brackets is left exactly as written, and this is the step that saves you
filing the bug:

- `[Solo]`, `[x2]`, `[N.C.]` — annotations. They print as typed and never move.
- `[Am F G Am (2×)]` — a bracket is read token by token, so the three chords
  move and the `(2×)` between them stays put.
- `[Amm]` — a chord you mistyped is not a chord, so it stands still while the
  song goes past it. This is usually how you find it.
- `\[C]` — escaped, so it is the literal text `[C]` and not a chord at all.

Inline chords are chords like any other: `[[Am F G]]` moves exactly as
`[Am F G]` does. And nothing outside a bracket is touched — a `G` in the lyrics
is a letter.

You do not have to transpose to find out which is which. The editor draws a
dotted underline under every bracketed token that is not a chord, and hovering
it says that the text prints exactly as written and that transpose leaves it
alone. It reads each name separately, so the `Gmimi` in `[Em Am Gmimi]` is
underlined on its own while the two chords beside it are not.

:::note[Not a complaint]
The underline is information, not an error. `[Solo]` and `[x2]` are exactly what
brackets that are not chords are for, and a song full of them is a correct song.
See [Chords](../songs/syntax.mdx#chords) for the whole of what counts as one.
:::

## 4. Undo

Because the transpose is a real edit to real text, it is undone like one:
`Ctrl` + `Z`, or the <Ui id="editor.undo" /> button in the third group of the
bar, <Ui id="editor.historyGroup" />. Redo is `Ctrl` + `Y`, or
`Ctrl` + `Shift` + `Z`, whichever your hands already know, and the button beside
it is <Ui id="editor.redo" />.

One press of the button is one undo, so a song you stepped up four times comes
back in four presses. That is what makes the two notes safe to experiment with:
press, look at the render, and take it back if it was not the key you meant.

The history belongs to the editor you are sitting in, and it lasts as long as you
are sitting in it. Leave the song and come back and there is nothing to undo —
so if the rewrite was a mistake, the way back is to transpose the same number of
semitones in the other direction (step 10 says what that does and does not
restore).

## 5. Choose the notation the chords print in

The gear at the end of the bar (<Ui id="editor.settings" />) opens the render
settings. The last row of their <Ui id="settingGroup.chords" /> section is the
one this step is about: <Ui id="setting.notation" />, offering <Ui id="setting.notation.english" />
or <Ui id="setting.notation.german" />.

This is not a transpose and it moves no pitch. It is the **alphabet the page is
printed in**: German calls B natural `H`, and keeps the plain `B` for B flat.
Only the printed page changes; the song is left exactly as you wrote it, which
is why you can switch it back and lose nothing.

The two meet in one place. A transpose has to write some spelling into the
source, and it writes the song's own: in a song set to German, a chord that lands
on B natural is written `[H]` and not `[B]`, so the text and the page stop
disagreeing with each other. A B flat is the exception and stays `Bb` in the
text — a bare `B` in a file is B natural to every reader whatever their setting,
so writing the printed spelling back would move the chord a semitone the next
time the song was read. It prints as `B` all the same.

The editor watches for the mismatch too: an `[H]` in a song set to English, or a
`[B]` in one set to German, gets a wavy underline saying which name the page
will print instead. Neither is illegal — it is a warning that the source and the
page are about to read differently.

Notation cascades like every other render setting, so a whole book can be
spelled one way at once:
[Build a songbook](./build-a-songbook.mdx#13-restyle-the-chords-for-the-whole-book).

## 6. While playing: open the transpose control

Everything from here shifts a screen and saves nothing.

Start a performance — <AppLink to="stage">Stage</AppLink>, then the songbook, or
a book's own Perform button; [Perform from a songbook](./perform-from-a-songbook.mdx)
covers that part. The control is then on the performing bar.

On a desktop it is in the open at the end of the bar's left half, ruled off from
the icons beside it: a note with a down arrow, the offset, a note with an up
arrow. On a phone the bar is four thumb targets and everything rarer lives
behind <Ui id="stage.menu" />, where the row reads <Ui id="transpose.title" /> and
opens a small sheet at the foot of the screen.

The sheet is deliberately not a dialog. It stays open while you step and it does
not cover the song, because the thing you are pressing the buttons to look at is
the song.

## 7. Step up or down, and read the offset

<Ui id="transpose.down" /> and <Ui id="transpose.up" /> move it a semitone at a
time, and the number between them says where you are: semitones, always signed,
`+2` or `-3`. It goes brand-coloured off zero, so a bar you glance at tells you
the set is not in the written key.

<Image
  alt="A performance transposed two semitones up: the stepper on the bar reading +2, and the chords under it moved"
  src="/img/screenshot-stage-transpose.png"
  srcDark="/img/screenshot-stage-transpose-dark.png"
/>

The page behind it is drawn by the very same rewrite the editor's buttons
perform — so the rules of step 3 hold here too, and `[Solo]` stands still on
stage as well — except that the rewritten text is thrown away as soon as it has
been drawn. Your song is not touched by it.

Eleven semitones each way is as far as it travels. Twelve is the same music an
octave away and therefore the same page, so the twelfth step lands on `0` rather
than pretending to be a setting that does something.

On a phone the menu row carries the number in its own label, so a menu you have
not even opened still admits that the last half hour has been running a tone up.

## 8. Play on — the offset holds across the songs

The offset belongs to the performance, not to the song you set it on. Turn the
page and it comes with you: a capo does not come off between numbers.

It ends when the performance ends — <Ui id="stage.exit" />. And it is
deliberately not stored anywhere: reload the tab mid-set and you are back at the
written key. Coming back to a library that quietly plays everything a tone up,
with nothing on screen outside the stage to say so, would be worse than coming
back to what is written.

## 9. In the audience: transpose your own screen

What travels to a lobby is the song **as written**. The performer's offset stays
on the performer's screen, and each viewer gets a control of their own — which is
the only sane answer, since a capo on one guitar is not a capo on another.

For a viewer the bar is at the foot of the phone: open <Ui id="stage.menu" />,
then <Ui id="transpose.title" />, and the same three buttons open over the page.

<Image
  alt="A viewer's own transpose: the panel over the page, and the chords already moved"
  src="/img/screenshot-audience-transpose.png"
  srcDark="/img/screenshot-audience-transpose-dark.png"
/>

It obeys everything in steps 7 and 8, and it is just as private: the performer's
screen does not move, the next viewer's does not either, and no song anywhere is
rewritten. The viewer's side of the evening is
[Play to an audience](./play-to-an-audience.mdx#10-transpose-your-own-screen).

## 10. Go back to the written key

On stage and in the audience the number is the way back. Press it
(<Ui id="transpose.reset" />) and the offset is `0` again — one tap instead of
eleven, which is why a control that can wrap needs it. At `0` it is dead rather
than hidden, so the two arrows do not shuffle sideways every time you arrive
home.

In the editor there is nothing to clear, because there is no offset: there is
only text you changed. Two ways back, then. Undo, while the history still has it
— that one is exact. Or transpose the same number of semitones the other way,
which returns every chord to the right pitch and most of them to the same
letters: `F#` taken up and back down comes home as `Gb`, the same note under its
other name.

## 11. Know which of the two is kept

|              | Editor: the notes, `♯` and `♭` | Stage and audience: the stepper  |
| ------------ | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| What changes | the song's own text            | the screen it is drawn on        |
| Saved        | yes, like any edit             | no, and never                    |
| Reaches      | everywhere the song appears    | the one device you pressed it on |
| The way back | undo, or transpose back        | press the offset                 |
| Ends         | never — it is the song now     | with the performance             |

So: if you always play it there, write it there. If it is tonight's key, or your
key rather than the performer's, step it on the screen and leave the song alone.
